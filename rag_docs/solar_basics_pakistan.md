# Solar Basics for Pakistani Households

General educational reference. Not a substitute for a qualified installer's site assessment.

## How a home solar system works

A home solar system turns sunlight into electricity for household use. Solar panels produce direct current (DC) electricity. The inverter converts it into alternating current (AC), which runs fans, lights, fridge, TV, AC, and other appliances.

In a common Pakistani hybrid setup, solar power first runs daytime loads. Extra solar power charges the battery bank and may be exported to the grid if net metering is approved. During load-shedding or at night, the inverter supplies selected circuits from the battery.

The grid is still important: it provides power when solar and battery energy are insufficient, and it receives surplus solar power where net metering is enabled. A good system balances four things: daily electricity use, panel production, inverter capacity, and battery backup.

Basic flow: **panels → DC protection/wiring → hybrid inverter → home loads**. Batteries connect to the inverter, while the grid connects through the utility meter and approved protection equipment.

## On-grid vs hybrid vs off-grid

An **on-grid** system mainly reduces daytime grid consumption. It may export surplus units under an approved net-metering arrangement, but a standard grid-tied inverter normally shuts down during a power cut for safety. It is suitable if your main goal is bill reduction and you already have a reliable backup arrangement.

A **hybrid** system includes batteries and can run selected essential loads during load-shedding. It is usually the most practical option for Pakistani homes that want both lower bills and backup during outages. The key is to decide which circuits are essential: lights, fans, fridge, internet, and a few sockets are different from running every AC and the water pump.

An **off-grid** system works without the utility grid. It requires enough panels, battery storage, and usually a generator for extended cloudy periods or high-consumption days. It suits remote locations, farmhouses, or places with no dependable grid connection, but it needs careful energy discipline.

For most urban homes, hybrid is the practical middle ground. Before choosing, make a written list of essential loads and the number of load-shedding hours you want to cover.

## Net metering in Pakistan

Net metering lets an approved rooftop solar consumer export surplus electricity to the distribution company. The exported units can offset electricity imported from the grid, subject to the applicable NEPRA rules, billing arrangement, and your distribution company's process.

In Pakistan, net-metering applications are handled through DISCOs such as LESCO, K-Electric, IESCO, GEPCO, FESCO, and others. NEPRA's 2026 Prosumer Regulations describe a net-billing arrangement in which the licensee bills the consumer after accounting for electricity generated and consumed by the prosumer. [web:16] K-Electric states that net metering is available for eligible consumers and lists solar PV capacity from 1 kW to 1,000 kW, with a maximum PV limit of 1.5 times the sanctioned load. [web:23][web:25]

A homeowner normally needs a sanctioned electricity connection, compliant system design, approved inverter and protection arrangement, inspection, and utility approval before the bi-directional meter is installed or activated. K-Electric's guidance also refers to a vendor checklist, inverter datasheet, and technical design meeting NEPRA and AEDB/PPIB requirements. [web:20][web:28]

**What the homeowner should do:**

- Confirm the electricity connection is in your name and the sanctioned load is correct.
- Confirm rooftop ownership or obtain the required owner/landlord permission; ownership or tenancy documentation can be relevant in net-metering processing. [web:30]
- Ask the installer for a single-line diagram, equipment datasheets, and a written scope of work.
- Submit the application through the correct DISCO process and keep copies of every submission and approval.
- Do not energise a grid-connected system before inspection and approval.

Rules, capacity limits, fees, and processing practices can change. Always confirm the current procedure directly with your DISCO before purchasing equipment.

Sources: NEPRA Prosumer Regulations 2026; K-Electric net-metering pages and application form; NEPRA net-metering records. [web:16][web:20][web:23][web:25][web:28][web:30]

## Battery types for Pakistani homes

**Lead-acid batteries** usually cost less upfront. Flooded lead-acid batteries require ventilation, correct charging, and periodic distilled-water topping according to the manufacturer's instructions. They are heavier, provide less usable capacity from the same rated capacity, and their life depends strongly on depth of discharge, heat, and charging quality.

**Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4)** batteries cost more initially but are generally better for daily solar cycling. They need little routine maintenance, are lighter, usually offer more usable capacity, and commonly have a longer service life when correctly matched to the inverter and battery-management system.

For frequent load-shedding and daily battery cycling, LiFePO4 is often the better long-term choice if the budget allows. For occasional backup or a limited budget, a good-quality lead-acid system can be practical, but it needs realistic expectations about lifespan and maintenance.

Pakistani heat is hard on batteries. Keep the battery area ventilated, shaded from direct sun, dry, and away from flammable material. Avoid mixing old and new batteries, mixing chemistries, or using incompatible inverter charge settings.

## Typical appliance wattages

Use these as **typical ranges**, not fixed values. Check the label or nameplate on your own appliance, especially for ACs, pumps, and freezers.

| Appliance | Typical running watts | Typical surge / starting watts |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ceiling fan | 50–100 W | 100–200 W |
| LED light | 5–20 W | Similar to running watts |
| LED TV | 60–150 W | Similar to running watts |
| Refrigerator | 100–250 W | 600–1,500 W |
| Deep freezer | 150–400 W | 900–2,000 W |
| 1-ton inverter AC | 700–1,200 W | 1,500–3,000 W |
| 1.5-ton inverter AC | 1,000–1,800 W | 2,000–4,000 W |
| Water pump | 370–1,500 W | 2–4 times running watts |
| Iron | 1,000–2,400 W | Similar to running watts |
| Washing machine | 300–800 W | 1,000–2,500 W |
| Microwave | 800–1,500 W | Similar to running watts |
| Wi‑Fi router | 5–15 W | Similar to running watts |
| Security camera/DVR | 10–60 W | Similar to running watts |

Inverter ACs usually use less power once running, but compressor start can still create a surge. Water pumps, refrigerators, and freezers are especially important for surge planning. During load-shedding, prioritise lights, fans, fridge, router, and phone charging before ACs and pumps.

## Common sizing mistakes

A common mistake is buying a large battery bank without enough panels or inverter capacity to recharge it. Batteries store energy; panels and the inverter determine how quickly that energy can be replaced.

Another mistake is ignoring surge loads. A 1.5-ton AC may run within the inverter's capacity but fail to start if the inverter cannot handle its starting surge. Water pumps and refrigeration compressors need the same attention.

Households often size a system using summer consumption, then find that winter sunlight hours, smog, cloud cover, and panel soiling reduce production. Panel direction, shading from water tanks or neighbouring buildings, dust, and heat also affect output.

People also forget small always-on loads: Wi‑Fi router, security cameras, chargers, standby electronics, and the inverter's own idle consumption. These can noticeably reduce overnight battery backup.

Finally, do not design backup for the entire house by default. Separate essential and non-essential loads, then protect only the circuits that genuinely matter during an outage.

## Maintenance basics

In dusty Pakistani cities, inspect panels monthly and clean them when dust, smog deposits, leaves, or bird droppings are visible. Clean early morning or evening, use water and a soft brush or cloth, and never work on a wet roof or during peak sun.

For flooded lead-acid batteries, check electrolyte levels according to the manufacturer's schedule and top up only with distilled water if required. Keep terminals clean and tight, ensure ventilation, and never add acid unless the manufacturer specifically instructs it. Do not open sealed AGM or gel batteries.

Have a qualified technician inspect the system at least annually—more often after monsoon storms, roof work, or repeated faults. The inspection should cover panel mounting, DC/AC wiring, connectors, earthing, breakers, battery terminals, inverter fans and vents, error logs, and charge settings.

Keep the inverter area dry, ventilated, and free from stored items. After rain, look for water ingress, corroded connections, or damaged cables from a safe distance; do not touch wet electrical equipment.

Sources: Manufacturer maintenance manuals; solar-industry maintenance guidance; installer best practice.

## Safety and red flags

Stop using the system and call a qualified electrician or solar installer immediately if you notice a burning smell, smoke, melted plastic, hot cables, discoloured terminals, sparking, or repeated breaker trips. Do not attempt to repair wiring, open the inverter, or bypass a breaker.

Call urgently if a battery is swollen, leaking, unusually hot, making strange noises, or smells chemical. Keep people away, ventilate the area only if it is safe, and follow the battery manufacturer's emergency instructions.

Other red flags include frequent inverter shutdowns, tingling or shock from metalwork, water ingress, damaged cables, loose panels, and error codes that return after restart. Solar systems include high-voltage DC and grid-connected equipment; observation and record-keeping are suitable homeowner tasks, but diagnosis and repair require qualified professionals.
